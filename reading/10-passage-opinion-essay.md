## Should Schools Replace Textbooks with Tablets?

As classrooms around the world become increasingly digital, one question keeps resurfacing: should schools abandon traditional textbooks altogether in favor of tablets? While the idea is appealing on the surface, a closer look suggests that a complete switch would do more harm than good, at least for now.

Proponents of digital textbooks point to clear advantages. Tablets are lighter than a backpack full of books, can be updated instantly when curricula change, and often include interactive features — videos, quizzes, and simulations — that static print simply cannot offer. For subjects like science, where visualizing a process can be more valuable than reading about it, this is a genuine benefit.

However, the drawbacks are significant enough to give pause. Studies on reading comprehension consistently show that students retain information better when reading on paper than on screens, likely because physical books offer spatial cues — page position, thickness, chapter breaks — that help the brain organize and recall information. Screens, by contrast, encourage skimming rather than deep reading, a habit that becomes especially costly when studying complex material.

There are also practical concerns. Tablets are expensive to replace when damaged or lost, require constant charging, and introduce a host of distractions — notifications, games, social media — that a printed book simply does not have. Schools in lower-income areas may also struggle with the upfront cost of equipping every student with a device, potentially widening rather than narrowing educational inequality.

None of this means digital tools have no place in education — clearly, they do, particularly as supplements for interactive content. But replacing textbooks entirely, before addressing these comprehension and equity concerns, would be premature. A more sensible path forward is a hybrid approach: using tablets to enhance specific lessons while keeping core reading material in print, at least until the research on digital reading comprehension catches up with the pace of technological adoption in classrooms.
