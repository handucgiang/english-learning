## Bài giảng: The Psychology of Habit Formation

> Good morning, everyone. Today, I want to talk about something that affects every single one of us, whether we realize it or not — habits. Specifically, I want to look at how habits form, and more importantly, why they're so difficult to change.
>
> So, let's start with the basic model that psychologists use to explain habits — it's called the "habit loop," and it has three parts: a cue, a routine, and a reward.
>
> The cue is basically a trigger — something that tells your brain to go into automatic mode. This could be a time of day, a location, an emotional state, or even the presence of certain people. For instance, if you always check your phone right after waking up, the cue is simply waking up itself.
>
> The routine is the behavior itself — in this case, checking your phone. And finally, the reward is what your brain gets out of it — maybe a dopamine hit from new notifications, or simply relief from morning grogginess.
>
> Now, here's the key takeaway: once this loop is repeated enough times, the behavior becomes automatic. Your brain literally stops needing to make a conscious decision — it just runs the pattern. This is actually incredibly efficient from an evolutionary standpoint — it frees up mental energy for more important decisions. But it also explains why habits are so hard to break through willpower alone.
>
> A study conducted at MIT in the late 1990s found that even when the reward was removed, the habit loop could still persist if the cue remained strong enough. This is why, according to the researchers, willpower-based approaches to breaking habits — like just "trying harder" — tend to fail long-term.
>
> So what actually works? Most researchers in this field now argue that the most effective strategy isn't eliminating a habit, but replacing the routine while keeping the same cue and reward. In other words, you don't fight the loop — you redirect it.
>
> To sum up: habits form through a repeated loop of cue, routine, and reward. And the most effective way to change a habit isn't to rely on willpower, but to identify the cue, and consciously replace the routine while still satisfying the same underlying reward.
>
> Alright, that's the core model — happy to take any questions before we move on to the next topic.
